The problem

Matching is not the hard part. Investigating breaks is.

Detection tells you a record disagrees. It does not tell you why, what to do about it, or who should do it. That work still lands on people, and it is where the hours go:

  • Switching among internal systems, broker and custodian portals, files, emails, and spreadsheets
  • Finding the information needed to explain a discrepancy
  • Determining the correct next step
  • Routing exceptions to the right person
  • Documenting why a resolution was approved

Worked example

Example: resolving a quantity break

A fund’s internal system shows 100,000 shares executed. A broker file shows 80,000. An updated allocation record shows the remaining 20,000 shares were separately booked.

  1. Break

    The discrepancy

    Internal system
    100,000
    Broker file
    80,000
    Difference
    20,000 shares
  2. Evidence

    What was retrieved

    • Allocation record showing 20,000 shares booked separately
    • Execution report indicating a partial fill
    • Prior cases with the same allocation pattern
  3. Recommendation

    What the platform proposes

    Likely a partial fill booked as a separate allocation. Recommended next step: confirm the allocation mapping and close the exception. No booking change is proposed.

  4. Human approval

    What only a person can do

    An operations analyst reviews the evidence and approves, edits, or rejects the recommendation. The case, its evidence, and the decision are recorded.

    Nothing changes automatically.

Human control

How it is designed

AI proposes. Deterministic controls enforce policy.

The reasoning layer and the control layer are deliberately separate. Model output is a recommendation, never an action. What is permitted, what escalates, and what requires approval are enforced by deterministic rules that sit outside the model.

  1. AI classification and evidence gathering
  2. Deterministic rules and confidence thresholds
  3. Authorized human approval
  4. Logged final outcome
